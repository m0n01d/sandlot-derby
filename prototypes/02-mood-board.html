SANDLOT DERBY
16-BIT MOOD BOARD

Desert Golfing’s restraint, drawn with a Sega Genesis palette. The bet: a home run derby that is one screen, one gesture, sixteen colours, and a sprite with three frames. Every study below is original and rendered live from a 16-colour palette line, at 320×224.

1. The direction in three constraints

ONE PALETTE LINEThe Genesis draws from 512 colours but a sprite layer gets 16 at a time. Commit to one line of 16 for the whole game. No gradients, no alpha, dithering only in the sky.
ONE SCREEN320×224 and nothing scrolls except the ball. The wall is on screen from the first frame, so the goal is never explained. Desert Golfing did this with a cup; we do it with a fence.
THREE FRAMESStance, contact, follow-through. Sega’s arcade batters had a dozen frames; the minimal read needs three and a hold on the last one. The ball and its trail do the rest of the animating.
ONE NUMBERThe only HUD is the last distance in a chunky scoreboard font, and it fades. No menu, no lives, no timer. Park number sits small in the corner like Desert Golfing’s hole count.

2. Palette: one Genesis line

Every colour is 9-bit legal: each channel is one of eight levels. That constraint is what makes 16-bit art look 16-bit; the “retro filter” people add later is mostly this, plus no anti-aliasing. Fifteen colours plus transparent.

Genesis colour is 3 bits per channel (values 0 to 7). Emulators map those to 0, 34, 68 … 238; hardware output was a touch brighter and less linear. Master System was 2 bits per channel, SNES was 5, Neo Geo 5 plus a dark bit. These hex values are the emulator mapping.

3. Screen study

The whole game on one 320×224 screen. Side view, like g0lf and Desert Golfing, because the physics stays legible and the wall is a real place. The classic Genesis derby camera sat behind the pitcher, which sells a batter but hides the flight. Toggle the options to test how much the screen can lose and still read.

320×224, palette line above, drawn each toggle. Nothing here is a texture: sky bands, stripes, wall and sprite are all rectangles and lines snapped to the pixel grid.STUDY 01

4. Sprite and motion studies

The batter is a silhouette with a cap and a skin tone, nothing more. That is both the minimal read and an honest 16-bit convention: Genesis sprites were often 3 to 5 colours on the character and spent the palette on the field. The swing is three frames, the bat is a rasterised line so it gets true jaggies at each angle.

Sprite sheet, 32×48 cells at 4×. Frames: stance, contact, follow-through, bat drop. Hold contact for two frames on a hit; hold stance forever between pitches.STUDY 02
Ball at 4 px with one highlight pixel, trail as every-fourth-frame dots, wall spark, landing dust. The trail is the animation budget; the ball itself never rotates.STUDY 03
Type: a 5×7 scoreboard face for the distance, 3×5 for the park number. Yellow on the wall green is the classic outfield-scoreboard read. “HR” flashes twice then clears.STUDY 04
Parks are three numbers, wall distance, height, and wind. Same palette, one colour swap for night. This is the whole “endless” content: seeded parks, forever.STUDY 05

6. Camera decision

7. What “minimal” means when it is pixel art

  • Desert Golfing had two colours. We have sixteen, so the restraint moves from colour count to colour discipline: every colour has one job, no colour appears in two roles.
  • No outlines. Genesis sprites often had black outlines to pop against busy fields. Our field is not busy, so the silhouette can sit directly on the grass with no outline, which reads more modern and more calm.
  • Dither is a spice. One checkerboard band per sky transition and nothing else. The moment dither appears on the grass, it becomes a texture game.
  • Sixty frames, three drawn. Smooth ball motion at 60 Hz next to a three-frame batter is exactly the 16-bit sports feel: the world is fluid, the people are stamps.
  • Type is the only decoration. The scoreboard number gets the chunkiest font on the screen. Everything else is small, one colour, and mostly absent.
