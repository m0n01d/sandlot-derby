THE CAMERA CUT

Two cameras: an at-bat view where the pitch comes at you and you react, then a hard cut to the wide view for the flight, with the numbers. This is not a new idea, it is the classic derby structure, and that is the strongest argument for it. The prototype below lets you feel it against the single-camera version.

1. The precedent is exactly this

World Series Baseball ’94 on Genesis ran its Home Run Derby from the catcher’s-eye view for the pitch and cut to a field view once the ball was hit. The Griffey SNES games did the same with a tighter batter cam. Flick Home Run!, the closest modern analogue, is the at-bat view only and never cuts, which is why its flights feel like a number rather than an event. So the two-camera design is the traditional one, and the one-camera side view was the Desert Golfing-flavoured departure.

WHAT THE CUT BUYSThe pitch becomes real input. Reaction, not aim. A 100 mph exit velocity earned by timing feels like hitting; a drag vector feels like launching.
A MOMENT OF CONTACTEvery 16-bit sports game had a hitstop: a freeze, a flash, then the cut. Here the freeze shows your slash through the ball and the swing angle it made, so that beat is literally where the game lives. The wide view alone never gets it.
WHAT IT COSTSA second scene, a pitcher sprite with a windup, a batter from a second angle, and the ball drawn at four sizes. Roughly doubles the art, still tiny.
THE MINIMALISM TAXDesert Golfing never cut. A cut is an edit, and edits are a voice. It is fine as long as the cut is always the same, always instant, and never a transition effect.

3. Prototype

Both cameras, 320×224, the mood board palette. The pitcher mixes fastballs, changeups and curves, and misses the zone about a third of the time. The zone is oversized for thumbs. Slice through the ball, Fruit Ninja style. Drag a line; if it passes through the ball as it arrives, that is contact. The direction of your slice is the swing angle and becomes the launch angle, its speed is the power. A slice that misses, a strike, or a ball just brings the next pitch, and nothing is ever an out. Toggle to wide only to feel what the cut adds. Flight physics is the same calibrated model as the research note.

Slice through the ball as it arrives. Slice upward for loft, flat for a liner. Faster is harder. space = a medium 27° slice through the ball.

4. What the prototype settles

  • Slice through the ball. Every finger movement is tested as a line segment against where the ball is, with a nine-pixel margin and 120 ms of lag forgiveness. The first segment that crosses the ball is contact. The swing angle is the direction from where the drag started to the crossing point, so the whole stroke counts, not the last wobble. Slice upward for loft, flat for a line drive, downward and you top it into the ground. Power is finger speed at the crossing, with drag length as a floor, and it is blended 40/60 with how close to centre the slice passed.
  • Misses explain themselves. A slice that misses freezes the frame and draws your actual trail with speed lines at its closest point, a pulsing ring where the ball actually was, a dashed line between them and a label such as HIGH OUT 7 IN or EARLY. A called strike or ball shows the ring at the plate so you see what you took. Inches are real: the 8-pixel ball is 2.9 inches. This is the Fruit Ninja lesson: the miss has to be legible on the object, not on a meter.
  • It is still one gesture, and now it is a familiar one. This is Fruit Ninja’s verb pointed at a pitch. Nobody needs the tutorial. The only thing Fruit Ninja never had is that the slice has a direction that matters, and the freeze frame exists to show it: a slash through the ball, the angle, and the power, for 0.35 s before the cut.
  • The cut must be instant. Any wipe or zoom turns a hit into a cutscene. The freeze ends on release, then one white frame and a hard cut.
  • Real-time flight is too slow. A 450 ft ball is in the air for over five seconds. Every classic derby sped this up or cut early. 2× feels right; 1× is in the prototype for comparison.
  • The wide view is the scoreboard. Velocity and angle appear the frame after the cut, distance ticks up under the ball, the final number lands big. No separate results screen is needed, which keeps the no-menus rule intact.
  • Misses go straight back. A swing and miss never cuts. That asymmetry is what makes the cut feel like a reward rather than a scene change.

6. In Swift

Two SKScene subclasses, or one scene with two root nodes and a hidden flag, both driven by the same pure state machine. The cut is SKView.presentScene(_:) with no transition argument. The state machine has exactly the five beats from the storyboard, plus miss. It knows nothing about nodes, which keeps it unit-testable like the Flight module.
